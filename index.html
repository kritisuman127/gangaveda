Twenty-eight winters of malaiyo, and counting

Ranjit Mishra opened Ganga Veda in the monsoon of 1998 with a single kadhai, six tables, and his mother's kachori recipe. The kadhai has been replaced twice. The recipe hasn't.

The haveli we sit in has watched the river for a hundred and fifty years. Its courtyard is where you'll find us most mornings, tearing kachori into sabzi while the boats fill up below. By evening the same courtyard turns into the best seat in town for the aarti, with a kulhad of chai in your hand and nowhere to be.

Pilgrims, students from BHU, boatmen on break, photographers waiting on the light. They all sit at the same tables and eat the same food. That was the whole idea.

One day at Ganga Veda

The river sets the timetable. We just cook to it.

  1. 5:30

    Aarti and the first chai

    The lamps go up on the ghat. We pour the first kulhads at the courtyard gate. No menu yet, just chai, and biscuits if you ask nicely.

  2. 6:15

    Kachori sabzi, hot from the kadhai

    The Banarasi breakfast. Dal-stuffed kachori, a sharp aloo sabzi with hing, jalebi on the side because it's Tuesday, or because it isn't.

  3. 12:30

    Baati chokha and a long lunch

    Baati baked on cowdung cakes the old way, cracked open and drowned in ghee. Bharta of roasted brinjal and tomato. Chhena dahi vada if you have room.

  4. 4:30

    Chaat hour

    Tamatar chaat, the one thing you can't get outside this city. Then a lassi in a kulhad so thick the straw stands up on its own.

  5. 6:45

    Evening aarti from the balcony

    Twelve balcony seats face the river. They go first, so book them. Thandai and rabri during the aarti, meetha paan after.
